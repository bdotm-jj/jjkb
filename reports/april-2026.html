I · THE TAKEAWAY

Half the time, a third of the volume

April was the lightest testing month on the books since the baseline window began in November. Only three projects passed through Cathy's queue, against an average of nearly two per month over the prior five months. The work that did come through, though, moved noticeably faster — averaging 4.7 days end-to-end versus 8.0 days across the baseline.

The headline driver is fewer retests. Only one of April's three projects — the Atrium personal-lines retest — needed a second round, and it closed in a single day. The other two were resolved in a single pass.

That efficiency, however, is partly an artifact of volume. With only three projects in the sample, one outlier — Scottsdale API, at ten days for its single round — distorts the average in either direction. Strip Scottsdale out and the remaining two projects average just two days each. Add it back and the number doubles.

The more durable signal is the R1-to-R2 reduction: where retesting did occur, defects resolved 77% faster than first pass, well ahead of the 44% reduction averaged across the baseline period.

IV · WHERE TO LOOK NEXT

Findings & recommendations

Findings

01
Volume was the story. Three projects vs. nine in the baseline window — the lightest pipeline month in five, limiting statistical confidence.
02
Cycles ran shorter. 4.7d average is a 41% reduction from baseline. Two of three closed in two days or less.
03
Retest discipline held. R1 → R2 dropped 77% on Atrium — well ahead of the 44% baseline rate.
04
One outlier shaped the math. Scottsdale API's 10d R1 doubled the monthly average single-handedly — consistent with prior API integrations.

Recommendations

→
Watch May & June intake. If April's low count reflects a real pipeline gap, queue planning in Q2 may need adjustment to avoid a back-loaded quarter.
→
Debrief Scottsdale. Capture the scope and complexity drivers behind the 10-day R1 so future API integrations carry an informed estimate.
→
Document Atrium's retest pattern. Two single-day rounds is the standard to write down — what defect-resolution path got us there, and how to replicate it.
